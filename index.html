He couldn't eat a paratha without watching himself eat it.

Saurabh is thirty. He lives in Gurgaon with his wife, writes code for a living, takes a small white pill every morning for anxiety, and pricks his finger each day to check his blood sugar. For as long as he can remember, a voice has narrated every moment of his life. I am eating. I am watching myself eat. I am watching myself watch myself eat. The observer never sleeps. It splits every meal, every meeting, every breath into a corridor of mirrors.

He loses whole days to a browser tab and knows it while it happens. His manager wants him to be independent; he cannot choose a library without terror. His wife goes to her parents for four days and comes back glowing. He believes he is dumb. He believes he ruins everyone's happiness. He believes he is already dying.

Then he finds a therapist. No couch. No notebook. No diagnosis. Two wooden chairs, a window onto a neem tree, and questions no one has ever asked him.

"Have you considered that the observer and the thought might be the same movement?"

"Who appointed the judge?"

"You are afraid of being well."

Over fifteen sessions, a strange and deeply human conversation unfolds: about anxiety that lives in the body rather than the mind, about a father who drank and a boy who learned to watch from a corner, about the word dumb, spoken once and ringing for twenty years, about the search for a self that may not exist, and about what happens when you stop trying to fix what was never broken.

This is not a self-help book. It is a novel about a man sitting across from another man, learning to look at his own life without flinching. It is about the courage it takes to be ordinary, and the extraordinary thing that happens when you let yourself be exactly where you are.

I

The paratha was going cold.

Saurabh sat at the small dining table in their DLF Phase 3 apartment, two chairs, a steel rack of spices, morning light cutting through the window grille, and stared at the plate. His wife had made aloo paratha with a side of curd, as she did every Tuesday. The chai was still warm. Everything was ordinary. Everything was in place.

He lifted his hand toward the paratha.

I am lifting my hand.

The thought arrived instantly, like a camera switching on behind his eyes. Not the hand lifting — the watching of the hand lifting. And then, before he could tear off a piece:

I am watching myself lift my hand.

And then:

I am watching myself watch myself lift my hand.

His chest tightened. The breath, which had been moving on its own a moment ago, now stuttered, caught between his ribs like something snagged. The paratha sat there, torn halfway, oil cooling on his fingers. The curd was untouched. The chai would go cold too. Everything would go cold while he sat trapped inside the mirrors.

That's how he thought of it: mirrors. Not two, not three, but an infinite corridor of them, each one reflecting the last, each reflection a little more distorted, a little more panicked. He was in there somewhere. Or maybe he wasn't. Maybe there was no one in the mirrors at all, just the reflecting, going on and on, and the terror of not knowing which reflection was him.

"Are you going to eat or just sit there?"

Priya's voice came from the kitchen. Not unkind, just morning-tired, the voice of a woman who had learned that her husband sometimes vanished while sitting right in front of her.

He didn't answer. He couldn't. The mirrors had him.

He pressed his thumbnail into the flesh of his palm, hard, a crescent of pain, and the corridor collapsed. Not gently. More like a screen going black. He was back. The paratha. The curd. The light through the grille. Priya rinsing a pan.

He ate mechanically, tasting nothing.

* * *

He'd been like this for years. Maybe always. The earliest memory he could locate was around eight or nine, lying in bed in the old house, staring at the ceiling fan, and suddenly thinking: I am thinking. And then: Who is thinking that I am thinking? And then the panic, vast and formless, like the ground had opened beneath the bed and there was nothing underneath, nothing at all, just the falling and the watching of the falling.

Nobody had noticed. He was the quiet one. His uncle called him slow — a word he would later learn was the softer version of what the man really thought. His teachers called him nothing at all, which was worse; they looked through him the way you look through a window that needs cleaning, aware of something there but not enough to stop. He failed every grade. Not dramatically, not the loud failure of a boy who fights or clowns or refuses. The quiet failure of a boy whose brain was running a different program entirely — scanning the room for danger, reading the teacher's mood, calculating the angle of light on the door to know what time it was and whether his father would be home yet. There was no room left for arithmetic. The brain was in survival mode, not learning mode.

His father drank. His mother survived, which is not the same as lived. Some nights she would stand in the kitchen doorway after his father had passed out, her face a mask of something the boy had no word for, and she would say things. Things like What is the point of any of this and I should have ended it long ago and once, in a voice so flat it had no floor, If I'm not here tomorrow, you'll manage. He was eight. He held these sentences the way you hold a burning coal someone has placed in your hands — not because you want to, but because dropping it seems worse. His brother, two years older, handled it differently. By sixteen the brother was drinking too, a carbon copy of the father, as if the house had a mold and every man who stayed long enough came out the same shape. The boy watched his brother pour the first drink with the same hands their father used, and something inside him — something that was trying very hard to remain intact — pulled tighter, went deeper, became the thing that would eventually sit in his chest for the rest of his life and watch, watch, watch.

The anxiety had been permanent since his teens, a thing that lived in his chest and paid no rent. By twenty-eight, he had diabetes — decades of cortisol flooding a body that had never felt safe, the pancreas finally giving out the way a bridge gives out not from one lorry but from ten thousand ordinary cars. Clonazepam helped. Half a milligram at night, sometimes a quarter in the afternoon. It didn't fix anything. It just put a pane of glass between him and the panic.

He was thirty and felt sixty. Work was a fog; he'd sit at his desk staring at Jira tickets, three hours would pass with nothing done, and the thought would surface: If I died tomorrow, who would notice? Not the dramatic thought of someone planning an end. The flat, bookkeeper's assessment of someone who had calculated his own value and kept arriving at zero. His brain had been trained on death — his mother's whispered threats to end it, his father's slow drowning in the bottle, his brother following the same current — and now it scanned for death in everything. A job interview: This will fail. A new skill: You'll never learn it. A plan for the weekend: Everything collapses. The neural pathway had been laid in childhood and every year of silence had paved it smoother.

Priya would notice. But even that certainty was corrupted: She would be free. Free of the silence that filled their apartment like gas, odorless and heavy.

He Googled it on a Thursday, sitting in the office bathroom. Therapist near me Gurgaon. Pastel websites, promises of healing and wellness. He scrolled past them. Near the bottom of the second page: no photo, no testimonials. Just an address in Sector 15, a name — V. Krishnan — and a single line: By appointment.

He stared at it for a long time.

Then he called the number.

II

The office was not what he expected.

He had imagined a leather couch, framed diplomas, a box of tissues on a side table.

Instead: a room. Two wooden chairs with thin cushions. A window onto a neem tree. A small table holding a clay pot of water, two glasses, and a plant he couldn't identify. The walls bare except for a framed photograph of a river, black and white, no caption.

He stood in the doorway, unsure whether he'd come to the right place.

"Come in. Sit."

The voice came from the chair by the window. The man sitting there was perhaps fifty- five, perhaps sixty. Thin, close-cropped grey hair, a face carved by years of being still. He wore a plain white kurta. No watch. No notebook. He looked at Saurabh the way one might look at rain, with open, unhurried attention.

Saurabh sat. The chair was harder than he expected. Traffic from below: an autorickshaw, a vegetable seller, the distant hum of the Metro.

For a long moment, neither spoke.

Krishnan poured water from the clay pot into one of the glasses and set it between them. He didn't offer it. Just placed it there, as if water was something that existed and that was enough.

"Why are you here?"

The question was simple. But he asked it with such unhurried curiosity, as if he had no script waiting for the answer, that Saurabh's prepared speech dissolved.

"I — " He stopped. Started again. "I have anxiety. I've had it for a long time. Since I was a kid, maybe. I take medication. Clonazepam. I also have diabetes, the doctor says from stress." He was reciting facts. Medical history. Safe territory. "I found your — I found your listing online."

Krishnan nodded. The information had arrived and been received.

"And what does this anxiety feel like? Not the word — the thing itself."

Saurabh opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again.

"It's like... watching myself. All the time. I can't just — do things. I think about doing them. And then I think about thinking about doing them. And then — " He broke off, frustrated. "This morning I couldn't eat breakfast. I couldn't eat a paratha because the moment I lifted my hand, I started watching myself lift my hand, and then watching myself watch, and it just, it goes on and on. Like mirrors."

"Mirrors," Krishnan repeated. Not a question. Not a confirmation. Just the word, held up to the light.

"Yes. And the more I try to stop watching, the worse it gets. Because then I'm watching myself try to stop watching. And that's another mirror."

"So you are watching," Krishnan said. "And then you watch the watching. And then?" He leaned forward slightly, not with clinical interest but with something Saurabh couldn't name. Something almost like fascination.

"Then I panic. The chest tightens. I can't breathe properly. And I just — I freeze. I can't move, can't eat, can't work. I'm just... stuck."

"Stuck inside the mirrors."

"Yes."

Krishnan looked at him for a long moment. Then he turned his head and looked out the window at the neem tree. A crow had landed on a branch and was making its ugly, honest sound. He watched it completely, without commentary.

"How long has this been happening?" he asked, still looking at the tree.

"Ten years. Maybe more. Maybe always. I just — I didn't know what to call it."

"And in those ten years, what have you tried?"

Saurabh exhaled. "Everything. Meditation — that made it worse. I'd try to watch my breath and start watching myself watch my breath. Mindfulness apps. Vedanta, Buddhism, neuroscience. The Default Mode Network. Metacognitive anxiety." He waved his hand. "Everything."

"You have looked for answers in science. In religion. In spirituality." Krishnan said it as a quiet observation. "What did you find?"

"Nothing worked."

The silence that followed was not empty. It was full, the way a room is full after someone has said something true.

"Perhaps," Krishnan said slowly, "nothing was supposed to work."

Saurabh stared at him.

"Perhaps the one looking for answers is the question."

The words landed somewhere below Saurabh's thinking mind, in a place he couldn't quite access. He felt them more than understood them, a small disturbance, like a stone dropped into deep water.

"I don't — what do you mean?"

Krishnan didn't answer immediately. He poured water into the second glass, for himself this time, and drank. Then he set it down and looked at Saurabh directly.

"You say you observe your thoughts, and this creates suffering."

"Yes."

"And when you try to stop observing, that also creates suffering."

"Yes."

"Have you considered that the observer and the thought might be the same movement?"

The room seemed to tilt slightly. Or maybe it was Saurabh's understanding that tilted. He opened his mouth, but nothing came out.

"I'm not asking you to believe this," Krishnan said. "I'm asking you to look. Right now. The anxiety that is here — in this room, in your chest — can you look at it without calling it anxiety?"

"I — what?"

"Don't name it. Don't call it anxiety, don't call it fear, don't call it a problem. Just — look. What is actually here?"

Saurabh tried. He turned his attention inward, and immediately felt the familiar lurch, the camera switching on, the mirrors assembling.

"I can't. The moment I look, I'm watching myself look."

"Yes," Krishnan said, and there was warmth in it — something more honest than sympathy. "You see? The looking and the thing that looks — are they two, or one movement creating the illusion of two?"

Saurabh sat with this. The traffic sounds continued outside: the autorickshaw, the vegetable seller, a dog barking somewhere. The neem tree moved in a breeze he couldn't feel.

"I don't know," he said finally.

"That's a good place to be," Krishnan said. "Not knowing."

Another silence. His hands were gripping the armrests. He loosened them. Noticed himself noticing, and the mirrors flickered at the edges.

"I should tell you," he said, "I've had this since childhood. My family was — it was difficult. My mother was — " He stopped. "Something in me broke. Or maybe something that should have formed didn't form. I've been carrying this so long I don't know what I am without it."

Krishnan listened. He didn't nod encouragingly. He didn't make soft sounds of understanding. He just listened, the way the neem tree received the crow, without opinion.

"You said something broke," he said. "Or that something didn't form."

"Yes."

"And you have been trying to fix this."

"My whole life."

"With medicine. With meditation. With understanding."

"Yes."

Krishnan was quiet. Then he said something Saurabh would carry out of that room and into the days that followed.

"We are not going to fix you," he said. "You are not broken."

Saurabh felt something in his throat. Not tears (he hadn't cried in years) but a pressure, as if something was trying to surface.

"Then what are we going to do?"

"We are going to look," Krishnan said. "Together. To look at what is actually happening, without the word, without the diagnosis, without the story you have told yourself about yourself."

"That's it?"

"That's everything."

Saurabh stared at him. There was supposed to be a diagnosis. A framework. Homework. A label he could carry home like a prescription. Generalized anxiety disorder. Metacognitive dysfunction. Depersonalization. Something to put in the box.

"You seem confused," Krishnan said. The possibility of a smile.

"I am."

"Good. Confusion means something has moved. The old answers are no longer fitting. That is the beginning."

He stood. The session was over. Forty-five minutes had passed. It felt like ten.

"Same time next week," Krishnan said. A fact, like the water in the clay pot, like the crow in the neem tree.

Saurabh stood. His legs felt lighter than when he'd come in, though he couldn't say why. Nothing had been resolved. The mirrors were still there, gleaming at the periphery. But something had shifted, not in the mirrors, in his relationship to them. As if, for a few minutes, someone had stood beside him and looked at them too, and the looking-together had made them less terrifying.

He walked to the door.

"Saurabh."

He turned.

"The paratha tomorrow morning," Krishnan said. "When the watching begins, see if you can look at it without the word."

* * *

III

The autorickshaw rattled down the Sector 15 road, dodging a Maruti, a cycle-wallah, a cow standing in the median with the patience of a saint. Gurgaon slid past: half-built towers, a tea stall with plastic chairs spilling onto the road, a billboard advertising Luxury Living above an open sewer.

He felt strange. He wouldn't say better, or understood, or fixed. Just — different. As if the furniture inside him had been moved two inches to the left, and he kept reaching for things not quite where he expected.

We are not going to fix you. You are not broken.

The words sat in him like a low hum. He'd spent so long being broken, building a life around the brokenness, that the suggestion he might not be felt almost offensive. Like telling a man who had limped twenty years that there was nothing wrong with his leg.

The autorickshaw hit a pothole. Gurgaon did not care about epiphanies. Gurgaon had potholes to offer.

That evening, Priya made dal and rice. They sat across from each other, her scrolling her phone, a silence they had evolved into.

He picked up the spoon.

I am picking up the spoon.

The mirror, assembling itself. The watcher behind the watcher, the chest beginning to tighten.

And then, faint but clear, Krishnan's words:

Can you look at it without the word?

The spoon in his hand. The dal warm. The watcher watching.

For half a second, he simply looked. Just — at the whole thing. The way you might look at a landscape without picking out any single tree.

The mirrors didn't shatter. They went quiet. Like a television muted, the images still moving, but the sound gone.

Then it came back. Everything reassembled. He ate the dal.

But his hand was steady. The dal tasted like dal — his mother-in-law's recipe, earthy and sharp with lemon — and for a moment there had been no one eating, just the eating, and that was not nothing.

It was two millimeters.

It was a beginning.

About the author

Saurabh Mishra is a software engineer in Gurgaon, India, where he has spent more than a decade building the mobile apps people carry in their pockets. Mirrors, his first novel, grew out of a long interest in the contemplative traditions and a longer interest in the ordinary question of what it is like to be a mind watching itself. He lives with his wife and their dog.

Questions readers ask

What is Mirrors about?

Mirrors follows Saurabh, a thirty-year-old software engineer in Gurgaon who lives with anxiety, diabetes and a voice that narrates every moment of his life: I am eating. I am watching myself eat. I am watching myself watch myself eat. He finds a therapist, Krishnan, who refuses to diagnose or fix him. Over fifteen sessions the two men look at what is actually happening, without the labels.

Is Mirrors a self-help book?

No. It is a novel. There are no exercises, steps or techniques. The questions the therapist asks are the kind that have no answer you can carry home, and the book is about what happens when you stop looking for one.

Who is Mirrors for?

Readers of therapy novels such as Irvin Yalom's When Nietzsche Wept, readers of Krishnamurti and the contemplative traditions, readers of contemporary Indian literary fiction, and anyone who has spent years inside their own head at a desk.

Is Mirrors based on a true story?

It is a novel. The main character shares the author's first name, city and profession, and the book sits on Amazon's Biographical & Autofiction shelf. How much of it happened is a question the book leaves to the reader.

Where is Mirrors set?

Gurgaon (Gurugram), India: a DLF Phase 3 apartment, a tech office full of Jira tickets and standups, a therapist's bare room in Sector 15 with a window onto a neem tree, and the market outside it.

How long is Mirrors?

About 46,000 words, 177 pages on Kindle, in fifteen chapters that follow fifteen sessions. Most readers finish it in two or three sittings.
